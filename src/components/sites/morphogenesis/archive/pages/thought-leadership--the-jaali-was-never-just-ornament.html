    In contemporary Indian architecture, the perforated screen is not a decorative inheritance. It is a climatic instrument: filtering sun, tempering glare, encouraging air movement and giving institutional buildings a quieter, more intelligent relationship with their environment.

    A jaali is often misunderstood because it is beautiful. Its delicacy invites the easy reading of ornament: stone lace, cultural memory, a decorative surface inherited from older traditions of Indian architecture. Yet that interpretation is incomplete. In the climatic intelligence of the subcontinent, the jaali has always been closer to a machine than an embellishment.

    It shades without sealing. It admits light without surrendering the room to glare. It tempers air without consuming energy. It allows the wall to become porous, not weak; protective, not defensive. Long before the contemporary language of passive design became widespread, the jaali had already answered one of architecture’s most persistent questions in hot climates: how does a building remain open to light and air while protecting itself from heat?

    That question has become urgent again. India’s demand for cooling is rising, and the facade has returned to the centre of the architectural and environmental conversation. A glass building can defer the burden to its mechanical systems, but it cannot avoid the physics. Every degree of heat admitted through the envelope must eventually be paid for, in energy, equipment, emissions and operating cost.

    A passive cooling facade works differently. It reduces the heat a building absorbs through form, depth, shade, porosity, air movement and thermal mass. It does not ask a machine to correct what the architecture failed to consider. In this sense, the jaali is not nostalgia. It is one of the most precise environmental devices available to Indian architecture, and one of the most misunderstood.

     

    The facade is where cooling begins

    The modern building industry often treats cooling as a services question. The architect composes the envelope, the engineer calculates the load, the system is sized, and the energy bill becomes the building’s long-term confession. In climates such as India’s, that sequence is flawed from the start.

    Cooling begins at the surface.

    The orientation of a wall, the depth of a reveal, the porosity of a screen, the width of a corridor, the mass of a roof, the reflectance of a surface and the velocity of air through an aperture decide how much work the mechanical system must later perform. By the time the air-conditioning plant is being selected, many of the most consequential decisions have already been made.

    A jaali works because it intervenes early in that chain. It does three jobs at once. First, it cuts direct solar radiation before it reaches the occupied wall. Second, it breaks daylight into a softer and more distributed field, reducing glare while preserving illumination. Third, by filtering air through smaller openings, it can encourage a more perceptible movement of air across the edge of the building.

    This is why the jaali cannot be evaluated only as a pattern. Its value lies in its section. The size of the aperture, the thickness of the screen, its orientation, its distance from the occupied space and its relationship with corridors, courts and vegetation all determine whether it performs or merely decorates. A thin applied motif may resemble a jaali; it does not necessarily behave like one.

    At its best, the jaali converts the facade from a boundary into a climatic mediator.

     

    From heritage device to contemporary performance

    The enduring relevance of the jaali lies in its ability to reconcile conditions that modern buildings often separate. Shade is usually treated as a reduction of light. Ventilation is often treated as a compromise with heat. Daylight is frequently allowed in at the cost of glare. The jaali resists these binaries. It creates a more nuanced threshold between exterior and interior.

    This is the intelligence that traditional Indian architecture developed across courts, verandahs, screens, stepwells, shaded plinths and thick walls. These were not picturesque devices. They were environmental systems refined through climate, craft and habitation.

    Contemporary architecture does not need to imitate them literally. It needs to understand their logic. At Morphogenesis, the jaali recurs not as a stylistic quotation but as a performative strategy. The question remains consistent: what should the aperture do before it becomes a pattern?

    This distinction matters. An active facade depends on powered systems, automation and equipment. A passive facade depends on geometry, orientation, material and air movement. The two are not adversaries. In India, most institutional and commercial buildings will still require some degree of active conditioning. But the order matters. Passive design should reduce the load first; active systems should be sized only for the reduced demand that remains.

    When that order is reversed, the building becomes dependent on machinery to solve problems the envelope should have prevented.

     

    Modern School, Vasant Vihar: the screen as shade, memory and restraint

    The proposed Golden Jubilee Block at Modern School, Vasant Vihar offers a more subtle reading of the jaali than the heroic desert case study. Here, the perforated screen is not asked to perform as a single climatic spectacle. It is part of a larger grammar of institutional continuity: red brick, exposed concrete, wide corridors, courtyards, shaded spill-out spaces and a measured response to Delhi’s composite climate.

    The project is planned as a Centre for Performing Arts within the eight-acre Modern School campus in New Delhi, on a compact 1,500 sq m site. The programme includes a multipurpose hall, black box theatre, music recording studios, activity rooms and administrative areas. The design brief is therefore both civic and intimate: it must accommodate performance and assembly, but also preserve the everyday informality of school life. 

    The climate studies are instructive. Delhi is identified as a composite climate with high daytime temperatures for six to eight months, high solar radiation, seasonal humidity and low wind speeds, making shading and induced air movement critical to comfort. The presentation notes that passive strategies can achieve thermal comfort for 60 percent of the year, with air movement, thermal mass, night purging and evaporative cooling all contributing to the microclimate strategy. It also records that solar shading through cantilevered slabs, supported by ceiling fans, can generate a microclimate 3–4°C cooler and create comfortable outdoor conditions for students for 60 percent of the year. 

    Within this framework, the jaali becomes one layer in a composite environmental system. The ground floor plan explicitly marks a perforated brick jaali wall on the north and west sides, while also identifying courtyards as devices for cross ventilation and daylight. That placement is important. The west is not merely a climatic problem; it is also an urban and acoustic edge. The site analysis notes that the west is obstructed by the Vasant Vihar Club, with no significant views, and that screening this edge helps create a noise barrier between the club and the school while also protecting the building from harsh western summer sun. 

    The screen therefore performs with unusual economy. It shades. It filters. It protects. It edits noise and view. It gives the building privacy without sealing it off from the campus. In a school, that last quality matters deeply. Architecture for children cannot be overly defensive. It must allow supervision, porosity, movement and informal occupation. The jaali allows the facade to be protective without becoming mute.

    The project’s architectural expression also pays deliberate homage to the existing Modern School language: red brick, exposed concrete, recessed openings, courtyards and wide corridors. In that sense, the perforated brick screen is not an imported feature. It belongs to the institutional memory of the campus. It takes the weight of tradition and makes it useful again.

    This is a mature use of the jaali. It is neither nostalgic nor decorative. It is a wall made intelligent by omission.

     

    The Lalit Suri Hospitality Institute: the jaali as pedagogical atmosphere

    At The Lalit Suri Hospitality Institute in Faridabad, the jaali operates within a different kind of institutional brief. This is not a school of performance, but a school of hospitality, where learning and atmosphere are inseparable. The building must function as an educational campus while also immersing students in the spatial codes of high-end hospitality.

    Set on a five-acre site with a built-up area of 2,50,000 sq ft, the institute is conceived as a modular, climate-responsive campus that merges education with the experience of luxury hospitality. Its programme includes a 400-seat auditorium, a 200-bed hostel, training kitchens, cafeterias, teaching spaces and public areas designed to operate as lived training environments. 

    The defining natural condition of the site is a dense grove of mature Neem trees along the northern edge. Rather than treating this grove as leftover landscape, the design organises the building volumes to protect and integrate it. The built form shifts and weaves around the trees, creating shaded open and semi-open spaces and strengthening the campus microclimate. Low building heights maintain a pedestrian scale, allowing the green and the built to remain closely interwoven. 

    Here, the jaali belongs to a broader choreography of shade. The project uses north-south orientation to minimise harsh solar exposure, opens generously toward the Neem grove for shade and filtered daylight, and introduces a traditional Indian brick jaali on the southeast to provide solar protection. The screen blocks glare while admitting air and soft light. 

    This is a significant distinction. The jaali is not only reducing heat. It is shaping atmosphere. In a hospitality school, atmosphere is not incidental; it is part of the pedagogy. Students are not simply taught hospitality as procedure. They inhabit spatial conditions that teach proportion, tactility, filtered light, shaded movement, pause, service, gathering and comfort.

    The brick screen participates in that learning. It moderates the southeast sun, but it also produces a softened threshold between classroom and campus, between training and inhabitation. Its filtered light is less clinical than a sealed glazed wall; its porosity allows the building to remain sensorially connected to the outside; its materiality anchors the campus in an Indian institutional language without falling into imitation.

    The environmental system is also layered. Cavity walls, parasol roofs, screens, pergolas and water features temper the microclimate along pedestrian corridors. Daylight simulations are used to ensure visual comfort inside classrooms, balancing illumination with glare control. Rainwater collected across the campus feeds spray ponds and water bodies along primary circulation routes, generating evaporative cooling and reinforcing the microclimate. 

    The jaali, in this context, is one instrument within a larger climatic ensemble. It does not claim authorship of the building’s performance alone. It works with trees, water, orientation, roof shade, wall depth and material robustness. That is precisely why it is credible.

     

    Two institutions, two readings of the screen

    Modern School and The Lalit Suri Hospitality Institute reveal the range of the jaali as a contemporary architectural device.

    At Modern School, the perforated brick screen is civic and protective. It moderates Delhi’s harsh west, filters noise from the adjoining club, preserves privacy, and aligns with the red-brick language of the existing campus. It is a device of continuity as much as climate. It allows a new performing arts block to belong to an older institutional fabric without surrendering its contemporary clarity.

    At The Lalit Suri Hospitality Institute, the jaali is atmospheric and pedagogical. It tempers the southeast sun, filters daylight into a softer register, permits air movement, and contributes to a campus where hospitality is learned through inhabitation as much as instruction. It gives the building a sense of refinement without excess.

    Both projects resist the superficial use of the jaali as motif. In each, the screen is situated within a larger environmental strategy. It is supported by orientation, courtyards, open corridors, shaded spill-out spaces, vegetation, water systems, parasol roofs, cavity walls and calibrated daylight. This is important because the jaali is most powerful when it is not isolated. It performs best as part of an architectural ecology.

    It is tempting to ask how much cooling load a jaali can reduce. The more serious answer is that the number depends on climate, orientation, aperture, material, cavity, programme and mechanical baseline. A universal percentage would be convenient, but not rigorous. The better question is what burden the screen removes before the building asks its systems to intervene.

    In both these projects, the answer is substantial. The jaali reduces direct exposure, softens light, preserves air, improves comfort at the building edge and extends the usability of transitional spaces. It allows the institution to breathe.

     

    The sophistication of restraint

    There is a particular discipline in allowing a building to be comfortable without making it hermetic. Much of twentieth-century institutional architecture equated comfort with sealed interiors and mechanical control. The result was often a narrow thermal expectation, fixed around an air-conditioned setpoint, regardless of climate, season or bodily adaptation.

    Passive cooling architecture asks for a broader understanding of comfort. It recognises air movement, shade, acclimatisation, surface temperature, humidity, material tactility and behavioural rhythm as part of the architectural environment. A shaded corridor with a breeze, a courtyard with filtered light, a jaali wall that cuts glare but preserves air, can produce a kind of comfort that is spatial rather than merely mechanical.

    This is where the jaali becomes especially valuable. It does not produce the sealed neutrality of a mechanically conditioned box. It produces modulation. Light moves. Air shifts. The boundary between interior and exterior remains legible without becoming harsh. The occupant remains aware of climate, but not oppressed by it.

    Architecture should not always seek to erase climate. In many Indian contexts, its more intelligent task is to edit climate: to soften it, delay it, shade it, direct it and make it habitable.

     

    Where the jaali has limits

    A serious argument for the jaali must also acknowledge where it cannot do everything.

    The device depends on precision. Poorly proportioned perforations can admit glare. A screen placed on the wrong orientation may underperform. A cavity without air movement can trap heat. A decorative jaali with no climatic calibration may look traditional while doing very little environmental work.

    Climate also matters. In hot-dry conditions, a jaali can support strong passive cooling strategies when combined with thermal mass, night purging and evaporative cooling. In hot-humid regions, it continues to shade and diffuse daylight, but the cooling effect of air movement is moderated by moisture-laden air. In composite climates such as Delhi and Faridabad, it must respond to seasonal variation: summer heat, monsoon humidity, winter coolness and changing wind patterns.

    Nor should passive cooling be romanticised as a universal replacement for air conditioning. In many building types, climates and occupancies, active systems will remain necessary. The value of passive design lies in reducing their burden, limiting their size, extending the hours of comfort, and lowering the building’s long-term dependence on energy-intensive correction.

    A good passive facade does not make engineering irrelevant. It makes engineering more precise.

     

    An old screen for a contemporary crisis

    The renewed relevance of the jaali is not sentimental. It is technical, environmental and architectural.

    India’s cooling challenge will not be solved by equipment efficiency alone. More efficient chillers may reduce consumption, but they do not question why the building admitted so much heat in the first place. The deeper shift lies in designing buildings that need less cooling before they are serviced. That means returning attention to orientation, shade, porosity, thermal mass, roof build-up, courtyard proportion, vegetation, water, and the first principles of envelope design.

    The jaali belongs within that larger discipline. It is not a decorative motif to be revived for visual identity, nor a heritage element to be abstracted into pattern. It is a climatic instrument whose value depends on proportion, depth, material and placement. In the right conditions, it can reduce heat gain and cooling demand. In all conditions, if designed intelligently, it can make the building’s relationship with light, air and heat more civilised.

    The future of sustainable architecture in India will not be built only from new technologies. It will also come from the careful re-reading of older knowledge systems that were never primitive, only patient. The jaali is one of those systems. It reminds us that architecture’s environmental intelligence does not always announce itself through machinery. Sometimes it is a brick turned sideways, a wall made porous, a corridor kept open, a classroom protected from glare, a student commons made cooler by shade.

    At Modern School and The Lalit Suri Hospitality Institute, the lesson is clear. The facade is not merely the face of the building. It is the first act of climate moderation.
