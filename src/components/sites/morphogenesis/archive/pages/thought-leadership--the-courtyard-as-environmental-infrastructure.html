The Courtyard as Environmental Infrastructure

Home / Thought Leadership / The Courtyard as Environmental Infrastructure

At The British School in New Delhi, Pearl Academy in Jaipur and The Lalit Suri Hospitality School in Faridabad, the courtyard is neither an ornamental void nor a nostalgic reference. It is part climate moderator, part circulation system and part social institution.

Table of Contents

    Architecture is often assessed through what it builds: floor area delivered, rooms enclosed, structures raised. The courtyard asks us to value what has deliberately been left unbuilt.

    Its apparent emptiness is deceptive. Properly proportioned, a courtyard manages solar exposure, admits daylight, supports ventilation, receives water, establishes visual orientation and creates a shared centre around which institutional life can gather. It performs many of the tasks now divided among mechanical, infrastructural and circulation systems, yet it does so through space itself.

    This is why the courtyard has persisted across climates and building traditions. It is not a fixed form but an environmental relationship between height, width, orientation, opening and enclosure. In one climate, it may store the coolness of night and protect occupants from the afternoon sun. In another, it may draw air through surrounding rooms. Elsewhere, it may become a shaded extension of the classroom, a social commons or a visible demonstration of how water moves through a site.

    Its familiarity can, however, encourage superficial use. A rectangular opening at the centre of a plan does not automatically constitute climatic intelligence. A court may become a heat trap if its proportions admit excessive sun, an airless well if it cannot release warm air, or an underused decorative enclosure if programme never enters it. Its performance depends upon the architecture around it and the life allowed to occupy it.

    The British School in New Delhi, Pearl Academy in Jaipur and The Lalit Suri Hospitality School in Faridabad employ the courtyard under distinct conditions. One accommodates a multicultural school within a constrained urban site. Another moderates the extremes of Rajasthan’s desert climate. The third combines environmental performance with the rituals and pedagogy of hospitality education.

    Across all three, the courtyard is not where architecture stops. It is where several of its responsibilities converge.

     

    An Absence That Works

    Environmental systems are usually understood as networks of equipment: pipes, ducts, pumps, filters, drains and controls. They remain largely invisible, concealed above ceilings or within service rooms, while maintaining the conditions in which occupation becomes possible.

    The courtyard proposes another model of infrastructure. Its principal components are not machines but dimensions. The height of the enclosing walls determines the duration of shade. The width of the opening regulates daylight and solar access. The disposition of corridors establishes whether air can move through adjacent spaces. Trees, permeable surfaces and water alter the temperature of the air entering the building. Verandahs and overhangs extend the period during which the edge remains habitable.

    These elements do not eliminate the need for mechanical services. They reduce the magnitude of the problem those services must solve.

    The same space also carries social and cognitive functions. It allows occupants to understand where they are. It gathers routes that might otherwise remain anonymous corridors. It brings collective life into view, turning movement through an institution into a sequence of encounters.

    This convergence matters particularly in schools. An educational building is not simply a collection of classrooms. Much of its culture is formed in the intervals between formal instruction: on stairs, beneath trees, along verandahs and around shared courts. When these transitional spaces are environmentally comfortable, they cease to be residual. They become part of the institution’s pedagogical territory.

     

    Delhi: The School Finds Room Between Rooms

    Completed in 2016, The British School occupies 5.3 acres in New Delhi and accommodates a built area of approximately 297,000 square feet. The redevelopment had to double the school’s capacity from 650 to 1,300 students while the existing campus remained operational.

    This constraint shaped the project fundamentally. The new school could not be conceived as an isolated object placed upon an empty site. Construction began around the functioning institution, with a perimeter block accommodating existing operations before the former building was vacated and replaced by subsequent phases.

    The campus that emerged is correspondingly porous. Courtyards, verandahs, terraces and semi-enclosed breakout spaces are carved from the built volume, creating an architecture in which movement and learning continually overlap.

    The school serves students from more than 55 nationalities. Workshops with teachers, administrators and student representatives between the ages of three and eighteen informed the brief. One of the most significant outcomes was the recognition that informal space required as much attention as the classroom.

    Breakout areas are therefore distributed along routes and thresholds rather than isolated as designated recreational rooms. Their occupation changes through the day: a corridor edge becomes a place for discussion; a shaded landing accommodates individual study; a courtyard supports performance, assembly or informal teaching. Such flexibility also makes efficient use of a constrained urban site. One space can serve several forms of learning without each activity demanding another enclosed room.

    The courtyard system provides the environmental structure for this informality. Larger volumes are positioned towards the south, shielding internal courts from intense solar exposure. The courts are scaled so that most remain shaded for much of the year, while chajjas and multiple-height verandahs protect their edges. Circulation is naturally lit, shaded and ventilated.

    Half of the school was planned to operate without air-conditioning, using passive measures to temper the environment. The importance of this decision extends beyond reduced energy demand. Students encounter seasonal variation as part of their daily movement through the building. Comfort is not presented as an invisible condition supplied uniformly to every space, but as something architecture produces through orientation, shade, air and vegetation.

    The landscape further expands the courtyard’s infrastructural role. Existing trees were protected or transplanted with the participation of students. Some congregation spaces take their cue from the chaupal and gather beneath mature tree canopies. Bioswales, rain gardens and a visible rainwater-harvesting system make the movement of water legible across the campus.

    Sustainability here is demonstrative without becoming didactic. A child need not study a services diagram to understand why one space is cool, where rainwater collects or how a tree changes the experience of a court. The building makes these relationships available through occupation.

    At The British School, the courtyard becomes an environmental classroom before it is used as a teaching space.

    Jaipur: The Courtyard Descends into the Earth

    Pearl Academy confronts a more severe climatic condition. Located in the industrial outskirts of Jaipur, the 215,000-square-foot institution is compact and introverted, protected from a hot-dry desert environment by an opaque perimeter and a perforated outer skin.

    Behind this controlled exterior, the plan becomes unexpectedly fluid. Internal courts curve through the building like a continuous spatial loop, drawing daylight into classrooms and studios while creating shaded routes between them. The contrast between outside and inside recalls the intelligence of traditional Rajasthani settlements: a defensive external form containing a more intricate and socially animated interior.

    In Jaipur, an exposed open space can rapidly become inhospitable. The courtyard must therefore be understood as a calibrated enclosure rather than an unqualified opening to the sky. Its depth, shadow and relationship to water are essential.

    Self-shading courts limit direct solar exposure while allowing diffused light to reach the adjoining studios. A double skin derived from the jaali stands approximately four feet from the main building envelope. Its perforation density responds to façade orientation, filtering air, light and views while intercepting solar radiation before it reaches the inner enclosure.

    The courtyard and the screen work together. The jaali moderates the building’s external boundary; the courts bring controlled environmental access into its centre. Neither operates as an isolated historical quotation. Their contemporary value lies in the climatic work they perform.

    The sequence culminates in the academy’s underbelly, a sunken social and exhibition space derived from the thermal logic of the baoli. The building is raised above an earth-banked level in which recreation, dining, performance and student gatherings can occur outside a conventionally air-conditioned interior.

    At approximately three metres below ground, the earth approaches Jaipur’s annual average temperature of about 25°C. Recycled water from the sewage-treatment system supports evaporative cooling, while the surrounding ground provides thermal stability. Project studies document internal temperatures of approximately 27°C when the exterior reaches 47°C.

    The twenty-degree differential is striking, but the more significant achievement is programmatic. The underbelly accommodates functions that might otherwise have required an additional enclosed floor. Environmental design reduces energy demand by changing the spatial category of the programme itself.

    The courtyard is consequently part of a larger thermal section extending from sky to earth. Shade limits radiant heat from above. The building mass protects the courts laterally. Water cools the air through evaporation. The ground moderates temperature below. At night, when desert temperatures fall, accumulated heat can dissipate.

    This is infrastructure in its most architectural form. No single component produces comfort. Comfort emerges from the relationship between enclosure, depth, material, water and time.

    Pearl Academy also demonstrates why the courtyard cannot be separated from institutional culture. The cooled underbelly is not a service zone hidden beneath the building. It is the academy’s social anchor, accommodating exhibitions, fashion shows and informal occupation. Environmental moderation enables visibility and collective life.

    The coolest part of the project is also among its most public.

     

    Faridabad: A Grove Becomes the First Room

    At The Lalit Suri Hospitality School, completed in Faridabad in 2019, the environmental order begins with something the architects chose not to remove.

    A dense grove of mature neem trees occupied the northern edge of the site. Rather than treating the trees as landscape to be arranged after the building, the plan moves towards and away from the grove, preserving its canopy and using it to shade open and semi-open spaces.

    The courtyard system grows from this relationship. Low brick volumes, landscaped courts, light wells and naturally ventilated corridors establish a sequence between the planted edge and the interior. The building does not contain a singular ceremonial court. It is organised through several protected openings and spill-out spaces that extend the forest into the institution.

    This distinction is important. The courtyard need not always be a geometrically complete room enclosed on four sides. It can operate as a thickened threshold between built form and existing ecology, provided its edges, shade and airflow are spatially defined.

    Faridabad’s composite climate requires the architecture to accommodate both hot-dry and humid periods. The building is oriented north-south, limiting the area exposed to difficult eastern and western sun. Brick jaalis protect the south-eastern envelope while allowing air and filtered light to pass. Cavity walls, overhangs, terrace gardens and cantilevered projections further reduce heat gain.

    Courtyards and corridor cut-outs support cross-ventilation and the upward movement of warm air. North-westerly summer winds are directed across water bodies to temper external spaces, while monsoon winds from the north-east and south-east assist air movement during more humid periods. Circulation is fully naturally ventilated, and project documentation records more than 90 per cent daylit and cross-ventilated built spaces.

    The performance figures are underpinned by a material discipline. Exposed brick provides thermal mass, durability and a readily available local construction system. Low window-to-wall ratios protect enclosed spaces from solar gain, while screens and recessed openings admit light selectively. The courtyard mediates between these relatively solid walls, preventing environmental restraint from becoming spatial isolation.

    The school’s programme adds another dimension. Hospitality cannot be learnt entirely through lectures. It depends upon choreography: receiving, serving, preparing, gathering and moving between public and private realms. The architecture is therefore designed so that spaces carry a dual purpose.

    Cafeterias operate both as dining spaces and food-and-beverage training environments. Kitchens allow students to prepare food while practising service. Hostel accommodation follows a hotel-room format. Open areas and courts become spill-over spaces where the social rituals of hospitality can be rehearsed beyond formal classrooms.

    Here, environmental and pedagogical flexibility reinforce one another. A shaded court supports informal study because it is thermally usable. The same space accommodates gathering because it is centrally accessible. Its proximity to training environments allows observation and service to become part of everyday circulation.

    Project documentation records an Energy Performance Index of no more than 58 kWh per square metre per year, compared with the cited ECBC benchmark of 90, alongside a 35 per cent reduction in energy consumption. The water strategy combines rainwater harvesting and collection wells, reducing freshwater consumption by approximately 30 per cent.

    These outcomes are not products of the courtyards alone. They arise from the combined work of orientation, cavity construction, shading, natural ventilation, daylight, water management and landscape. The courtyard is the space through which these systems encounter one another.

    At Lalit Suri, it is also where an education in hospitality becomes visible as a shared way of life.

     

    The Mathematics of Shade

    The recurrence of the courtyard across Indian architecture can make it appear almost instinctive. Yet its success depends upon decisions that are highly exact.

    A court that is too broad may receive excessive summer radiation. One that is too narrow may struggle to provide adequate daylight at its lower levels. Its proportions must respond to solar altitude, building height and the orientation of surrounding rooms. Openings must be positioned so that air has both an entry and an exit. Vegetation must provide shade without obstructing useful wind. Water assists evaporative cooling in dry conditions, but its effect diminishes as humidity rises.

    Programme introduces another set of requirements. A learning court needs acoustic tolerance, visibility, access and places to sit. A circulation court must remain legible during periods of high movement. A planted court requires sufficient soil, drainage and light. An assembly court must accommodate occupation without compromising the environmental role of its permeable surfaces.

    There is therefore no universal courtyard module. Even within the three projects, the environmental operation changes.

    In Delhi, shaded courts mediate a composite climate and create additional learning territory on a constrained site. In Jaipur, a more introverted sequence protects occupants from extreme desert heat and extends downward into the thermal stability of the earth. In Faridabad, the courts negotiate between brick buildings and a preserved tree canopy, supporting seasonal ventilation and the social choreography of hospitality education.

    Their similarity lies not in form, but in principle: the unbuilt centre is given work to do.

     

    When Infrastructure Becomes Culture

    The courtyard occupies a rare position in architecture. It can improve environmental performance without retreating from human experience. A duct may move air efficiently, but it does not ordinarily become a place for conversation. A drain may carry rainwater, but it does not necessarily reveal the seasonal life of a campus. A light fitting illuminates a room, but it cannot reproduce the changing character of daylight across a shaded wall.

    The courtyard makes performance inhabitable.

    At The British School, students gather within the same spaces that demonstrate shade, ventilation and water stewardship. At Pearl Academy, the thermal strategy supports exhibitions and the creative life of the institution. At The Lalit Suri Hospitality School, climatic transition spaces become settings in which service, sociability and observation are practised.

    This is why the courtyard should not be reduced to an image of tradition. Its enduring value is not that it recalls an earlier architecture, but that it reunites responsibilities contemporary design has allowed to drift apart. Climate, circulation, landscape, learning and community can occupy the same spatial structure.

    For institutions, this integration is especially consequential. Education takes place through the culture of a building as much as through its designated rooms. An architecture that places collective life at its environmental centre teaches something about interdependence: between individuals and communities, buildings and seasons, comfort and resource use.

    The courtyard gives form to that relationship. It is not the space remaining after construction. It is infrastructure built from the decision not to build.
